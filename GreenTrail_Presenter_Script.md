# GreenTrail leadership presentation – presenter script

## Slide 1: Promotions that last: what worked in 2023 and what we test next

Thanks for having me. Today I want to answer one question: how effective were our 2023 in-store promotions, and what should we do differently? I’ll give you the answer first, show the evidence, then propose a strategy and a test that proves it before we scale. About 15 minutes, then discussion.

## Slide 2: Promotions bring people in, but only events bring them back

Three findings. One: promotions clearly work in the moment, a 34% sales lift, but almost all of it disappears once they end. Two: the format matters most after the promotion. Special Events kept sales 9% above where they started; Buy-One-Get-One ended below where it started. Three, the surprise: the strongest predictor of lift is how much spare capacity a store has, not which promotion it ran. So my recommendation is to retire BOGO, make community events our core format with a trade-in and repair credit, and prove it with a controlled test before we roll it out.

## Slide 3: 25 stores, one two-week promotion each, spring and summer 2023

A quick word on the data. Twenty-five stores, each ran exactly one promotion of about two weeks between April and August 2023. For each we have weekly sales and daily visits before, during and after. From that I calculated lift, spend per visit, how much of the lift survived, and each store’s sales per square foot before the promotion, which turns out to matter a lot.

## Slide 4: Every promotion type lifted sales, mostly by bringing more people in

During the promotion, everything worked. Sales up 34 percent, highly significant. Most of that is traffic: visits up 26 percent, while spend per visit rose only about 8 percent. Urban stores responded noticeably more than suburban ones. And if we only looked at the promotion window, we would conclude all three types are about equal. The real story is what happens next.

## Slide 5: Special Events kept sales 9% above baseline; BOGO fell below it

This is the slide that matters most. All three lines rise together during the promotion. Afterwards they split. Special Events stayed 9 percent above baseline, and not a single event store fell below where it started. Visits stayed 13 percent higher, so events are building habits. BOGO did the opposite: half the BOGO stores sold less after the promotion than before. That is pull-forward: people stock up, then stop buying. For a brand built on buying less and buying better, BOGO is also the wrong message. The after-period difference is borderline significant, p of 0.056, which is why I want to confirm it with a proper test.

## Slide 6: Stores with spare capacity gained the most

Here’s the finding I didn’t expect. Each dot is a store. Across the bottom is how busy the store was before the promotion, in weekly sales per square foot. The pattern is strong: quieter stores lifted most. New York, our least busy store per square foot, lifted 67 percent. El Paso and San Antonio, our busiest, barely moved. For every extra dollar of baseline sales per square foot, lift drops about 3 points. Once you account for that and for urban versus suburban, which promotion type a store ran makes no difference during the promotion. The practical implication: put promotional money where stores have room to grow.

## Slide 7: A few stores stand out, and the data has limits we must fix

Two things to flag. On the left, the stores worth a conversation with the regional teams: Philadelphia is our best lasting result and worth copying; Los Angeles had the worst post-promotion drop; Chicago brought people back but they spent less. On the right, the honest caveats. Every store promoted, so we have no true control. Each store ran only one type. Dates were spread across the season. And we have no margin data, so I can’t tell you which promotion made the most money. That’s exactly why the next step is a controlled test, not a rollout.

## Slide 8: Four moves that reward durable choices, not extra consumption

So what do we do? Four moves. Stop BOGO: it performs worst and conflicts with our values. Lead with community events, which I’m calling Trail Community Days: repair clinics, skills workshops, clean-ups, gear swaps. These had the best lasting effect and cost us no margin on price. Add a ReGear credit: trade in old gear or get something repaired, and get credit to spend on our durable and recycled lines within 30 days. That gives people a reason to come back and moves them toward the products we want to sell. And target the money where stores have spare capacity. Where we still use discounts, keep them shallow and on sustainable lines or end-of-season stock that would otherwise be wasted.

## Slide 9: To judge profit and cause, we need five more kinds of data

To move from signals to decisions we need more data. The most important gap is margin: without cost and discount depth we can’t say which promotion made money. Longer weekly sales history separates real lift from seasonality. Loyalty data tells us if we’re winning new customers or just discounting regulars. Demographics and local economics help explain why urban stores respond more. And sustainability metrics let us show the strategy is doing what we say. Outdoor retail is also very exposed to weather, local events and competitors, so we’ll log those and design the test so they hit every group equally.

## Slide 10: Matched stores, randomly assigned, all on the same calendar

How do we know the strategy works? A proper control group. We group the 25 stores into threes that look alike: same store type, similar sales per square foot, which we now know is the biggest driver of lift. Within each three, we randomly assign one store to Community Days, one to Event plus ReGear, and one to no promotion. All stores run on the same weeks, so weather, holidays and season hit everyone equally. We measure each store against its own baseline, and the effect is the test stores’ change minus the control stores’ change. The groups come out well balanced. This fixes the biggest weakness of the 2023 data, where we had no untreated stores to compare against.

## Slide 11: 14 weeks: 4 of baseline, 6 live, 4 of follow-up

How long? Fourteen weeks in total. Four weeks of normal trading to set each store’s baseline, six weeks with the promotions live, and four weeks of follow-up with promotions off. Why six weeks live and not two like in 2023? Two weeks shows the novelty spike, not whether people keep coming back. Six weeks gives three events per store and enough weekly readings to detect a 7 to 10 point difference, based on an assumption about weekly variation that we’ll confirm with real sales data. The follow-up is not optional: it’s where BOGO’s problem showed up. And we keep agility: a checkpoint in week three of the test lets us stop anything clearly losing, and results land in early June, in time for peak season.

## Slide 12: We scale an arm only if it wins on profit, lasts, and is greener

Here’s how we’ll decide, agreed up front so nobody argues afterwards. Three primary tests: the arm has to make more gross margin than control, lift sales by at least 10 percent, and not drop below baseline in the follow-up weeks. Then we check repeat visits, the share of sales from our durable and recycled lines, trade-ins and repairs, and make sure returns don’t go up. We’ll break results out by store type and by how busy the store is, and when we roll out, we keep 20 percent of stores as a standing control so we keep learning.

## Slide 13: Approve a 14-week test of community events and ReGear, starting March 2027

So, the ask. Approve the 14-week test starting in March. Pause new BOGO promotions in the meantime. Confirm the store assignment and name an owner for events in each test store. Get us margin, weekly sales and loyalty data so we can finalise the design and confirm the sample size. And agree today how we’ll judge success. Happy to take questions.
